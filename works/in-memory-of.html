In Memory Of

In a park by the river there is a bench with a small brass plaque screwed to its backrest, like the others along the path. The others say who. Mine says nothing.

I paid for it the way anyone does. There was a form, and on the form a box for the words, with room for three lines. I left the box empty. In time the bench came, and the plaque on it, polished and blank.

A memorial bench is a strange kind of chair. It asks you to sit in the place of someone who used to sit there, or liked the view, or is simply missed. You read the name, you count the years between the dates, and you rest for a while inside a stranger's loss.

In November 1920 Britain buried one soldier in Westminster Abbey whose name no one knew. He was chosen from several bodies so that no one ever could know it. A grave with no name could be anyone's, so it became everyone's.

This bench has no one at all. It does not tell you who to miss. People sit on it and look at the water and bring their own dead with them, or no one, or a dog. The plaque gives them nothing to read, so they go on thinking about whatever they came with.

In April someone tied a bunch of daffodils to the armrest with string. I do not know who they were for. The plaque did not say.
